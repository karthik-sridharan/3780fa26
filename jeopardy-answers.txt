CATEGORY: Supervised Learning & Model Selection
10) Hold out a <strong>validation set</strong>: train on the rest, score each candidate setting on the held-out part, keep the winner. Or <strong>$k$-fold cross-validation</strong>: split the training data into $k$ folds, train on $k-1$ and validate on the remaining one, rotate, and average. Either way the rule is the same &mdash; the hyper-parameter is chosen on data the fit never saw.
20) <strong>False.</strong> Training error on its own says nothing about test error. A lookup table (or 1-NN) has 0% training error and can be at chance on new data. Only an estimate on held-out data, or a generalization bound that controls the capacity of the model class, lets you say anything about test error.
30) Training error falls monotonically as $\lambda\to 0$ &mdash; less regularization never hurts the fit &mdash; so it is minimized at $\lambda=0$, i.e. no regularization at all. A quantity the model was fitted to cannot also referee it.
40) This is overfitting, so in both cases you want <em>less</em> capacity.<ol><li><strong>Decrease $C$.</strong> $C$ multiplies the loss term ($C=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda n}$), so a large $C$ means weak regularization. Lowering $C$ raises $\lambda$ and shrinks $\mathbf{w}$.</li><li><strong>Increase $k$.</strong> $k=1$ fits every point exactly; averaging over more neighbours smooths the decision boundary.</li></ol>
50) Split the 100 points into 5 folds of 20. For <em>each</em> of the 5 values of $\lambda$: train on 4 folds (80 points), measure error on the held-out fold (20 points), rotate through all 5 folds, and average those 5 numbers &mdash; that average is the CV error of that $\lambda$. So <strong>25 model fits</strong> in all. Pick the $\lambda$ with the lowest average, then <strong>refit on all 100 points</strong> with it. The CV number itself is optimistically biased (you selected on it), so report error on a separate test set.

CATEGORY: k-NN & Perceptron
10) That points close together in feature space tend to share labels &mdash; the labeling function is locally smooth with respect to the chosen metric. Everything hinges on that metric being meaningful.
20) $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{x}$ (and $b\leftarrow b+1$ if there is a bias). In general $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow\mathbf{w}+y\,\mathbf{x}$, which here has $y=+1$.
30) <strong>$O(nd)$.</strong> One Euclidean distance costs $O(d)$, and you need all $n$ of them, so $O(nd)$; then picking the $k$ smallest costs $O(n\log k)$ with a heap (or $O(n)$ by selection, $O(n\log n)$ if you just sort), and the vote itself is $O(k)$. Since $d$ normally dominates $\log k$, the distances are the whole bill: $$O(nd+n\log k)=O(nd)$$ Note where the cost sits: training is free (you just store the data, $O(nd)$ memory), and <em>every single test point</em> pays $O(nd)$. A linear classifier trains once and then costs $O(d)$ per test point &mdash; no dependence on $n$ at all.
40) The <strong>majority label of the whole training set</strong> &mdash; the same constant prediction for every input, since every point is one of the $n$ "neighbours" no matter where you query. This is the extreme underfitting end of the $k$ knob.
50) Pairwise distances <strong>concentrate</strong> &mdash; every point becomes roughly equidistant from every other, so &lsquo;nearest&rsquo; stops meaning anything and $k$-NN degrades. The distance to the hyperplane is <strong>unaffected</strong>: it depends only on the component along $\mathbf{w}$, and the added random coordinates are orthogonal to it. This is exactly why linear classifiers survive high dimensions and $k$-NN does not.

CATEGORY: Linear Methods and ERM
10) $$\min_{\mathbf{w}}\ \underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell\big(h_{\mathbf{w}}(\mathbf{x}_i),y_i\big)}_{\text{training loss}}\;+\;\lambda\underbrace{R(\mathbf{w})}_{\text{regularizer}}$$ The first term fits the data, the second penalizes complexity, and $\lambda$ trades them off. Choosing $\ell$ and $R$ gives you least squares, ridge, logistic regression or the SVM.
20) <strong>$d+1$ dimensions.</strong> Append a constant feature: $\mathbf{x}'=(\mathbf{x},1)$ and $\mathbf{w}'=(\mathbf{w},b)$. Then $\mathbf{w}'^\top\mathbf{x}'=\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}+b$, so a homogeneous classifier in $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ represents any non-homogeneous one in $\mathbb{R}^{d}$.
30) <ol><li><strong>Logistic regression: no.</strong> The likelihood has no closed-form maximizer; it needs an iterative method.</li><li><strong>Least squares: yes.</strong> $\mathbf{w}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \mathbf{y}$.</li></ol>
40) Take $-\log$ of (likelihood $\times$ prior): $$\begin{aligned}&\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(y_i-\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w})^2}{2\sigma^2}+\frac{\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2}{2\eta^2}\\[4pt]\Longleftrightarrow\quad&\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\mathbf{x}_i^\top\mathbf{w})^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{\eta^2}\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2\end{aligned}$$ So $\ell$ is the <strong>squared loss</strong>, $R(\mathbf{w})=\|\mathbf{w}\|_2^2$, and $\lambda=\sigma^2/\eta^2$ &mdash; this is <strong>ridge regression</strong>. A Gaussian prior <em>is</em> $L_2$ regularization; a tighter prior (small $\eta$) means more regularization.
50) <ol><li><strong>Closed form</strong> $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbf{y}$: $d$ small enough to form and invert $X^\top X$ (about $O(nd^2+d^3)$), and $X^\top X$ well conditioned. Exact, one shot.</li><li><strong>Gradient descent:</strong> $d$ large, so the $O(d^3)$ inverse is out of reach, but full passes over the data are affordable.</li><li><strong>SGD:</strong> $n$ very large or the data is streaming, so even one full gradient is too expensive &mdash; you want progress after a handful of examples.</li></ol>

CATEGORY: SVM
10) <strong>False.</strong> Only the <em>support vectors</em> matter. A point strictly outside the margin has $\alpha_i=0$; move it (or delete it) and the solution is unchanged.
20) <strong>False.</strong> A hard margin requires the training data to be <strong>linearly separable</strong>: if it is not, the constraints $y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i+b)\ge 1$ have no feasible solution at all. That infeasibility is exactly why we add the slack variables $\xi_i$ and move to the soft-margin SVM.
30) A support vector is a training point with $\alpha_i\neq 0$ &mdash; it sits on the margin (or violates it, in the soft-margin case). For a non-support vector $\alpha_i=0$, so it contributes nothing to $\mathbf{w}=\sum_i\alpha_i y_i\mathbf{x}_i$ and could be deleted without changing the answer.
40) <strong>A: $C=0.0001$ &mdash; B: $C=1$ &mdash; C: $C=100$.</strong> $C$ is the price of a margin violation, so the margin narrows as $C$ grows.<ol><li><strong>A</strong> has no visible margin lines at all &mdash; they are so far apart that they fall off the plot. With $C$ tiny the hinge term barely matters, $\|\mathbf{w}\|\to 0$ and the margin $1/\|\mathbf{w}\|$ blows up; the boundary is set by the bulk of the data (nearly vertical) and ignores the lone $\times$ sitting among the circles.</li><li><strong>B</strong> is in between: a wide margin with that one $\times$ allowed to sit inside it (the red segment is its slack).</li><li><strong>C</strong> has the narrowest margin &mdash; violations are expensive, so the boundary is dragged over to deal with the outlier.</li></ol> Measured perpendicular margin widths: B $\approx 2.6$, C $\approx 2.2$, A off-scale. Remember $C=\tfrac{1}{2\lambda n}$: large $C$ = weak regularization = narrow margin.
50) <strong>Yes &mdash; always, for any data and any labelling.</strong> Those $n$ new coordinates make the points linearly independent, so take $\mathbf{w}$ to be zero on the original features and $y_i$ on the coordinate belonging to example $i$. Then $$y_i(\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}'_i)=y_i\cdot y_i=1\ \ge 1\quad\text{for every }i$$ so every hard-margin constraint holds and the problem is feasible (margin $1/\sqrt{n}$). Compare the 20-point clue: a hard margin normally needs separability, and this trick manufactures separability for free. That is exactly why it is a terrible idea &mdash; the classifier memorizes the training set like a lookup table and has learned nothing about a new $\mathbf{x}$, whose new features are all $0$.

CATEGORY: Kernel Methods
10) <strong>False</strong> &mdash; that is the whole point. You only ever need $k(x,x')=\phi(x)^\top\phi(x')$. The map $\phi$ may be enormous or infinite-dimensional and is never formed.
20) <strong>Only (b).</strong><ol><li>$\vec a+\vec b$ &mdash; <strong>no</strong>. It is not even a scalar for vector inputs, and in one dimension $k(-1,-1)=-2<0$, while a kernel must have $k(a,a)=\|\phi(a)\|^2\ge 0$.</li><li>$\vec a\cdot\vec b$ &mdash; <strong>yes</strong>, the linear kernel, with $\phi(x)=x$.</li><li>$\vec a-\vec b$ &mdash; <strong>no</strong>. It is antisymmetric, $k(\vec a,\vec b)=-k(\vec b,\vec a)$, and a kernel must be symmetric.</li></ol>
30) $\phi$ gains one extra constant coordinate: $\phi(\mathbf{x})=(\mathbf{x},1)$, since $(\mathbf{x},1)\cdot(\mathbf{x}',1)=\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{x}'+1$. That is exactly the homogeneous-coordinates trick &mdash; adding 1 to the kernel buys the classifier a <strong>bias term</strong>. More generally $k+c$ appends the constant feature $\sqrt{c}$.
40) $k$ must be <strong>symmetric</strong> and <strong>positive semi-definite</strong>: for <em>every</em> finite set $x_1,\dots,x_n$ the matrix $K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)$ has no negative eigenvalue. Without building $\phi$: assemble $k$ from known kernels using the construction rules &mdash; sums, positive scalings, products, exponentials, $f(x)k(x,x')f(x')$, and polynomials with positive coefficients.
50) Expand the square and every term becomes a kernel evaluation: $$\begin{aligned}\|\phi(x)-\mu\|^2&=\phi(x)\cdot\phi(x)-2\,\phi(x)\cdot\mu+\mu\cdot\mu\\[4pt]&=k(x,x)-\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}k(x,x_i)+\frac{1}{n^2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}k(x_i,x_j)\end{aligned}$$ then take the square root. The double sum does not depend on $x$, so you compute it once ($O(n^2)$) and each new query costs only $O(n)$ kernel evaluations. $\phi$ is never formed.

CATEGORY: Gradient Descent
10) $$\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)}=\mathbf{w}^{(t)}-\eta\,\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})$$ Among all unit directions $\mathbf{u}$, the directional derivative $\nabla f(\mathbf{w})\cdot\mathbf{u}$ is <em>smallest</em> at $\mathbf{u}=-\nabla f(\mathbf{w})/\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w})\|$, so $-\nabla f$ is the direction of <strong>steepest descent</strong>. $\eta$ is the step size, and a point with $\nabla f=0$ is a fixed point of the update.
20) $f$ is $L$-smooth if its gradient is $L$-Lipschitz: $$\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w})-\nabla f(\mathbf{w}')\|\le L\|\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{w}'\|\quad\text{for all }\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}'$$ equivalently $f(\mathbf{w}')\le f(\mathbf{w})+\nabla f(\mathbf{w})\cdot(\mathbf{w}'-\mathbf{w})+\tfrac{L}{2}\|\mathbf{w}'-\mathbf{w}\|^2$ &mdash; $f$ sits under a quadratic of curvature $L$, so the gradient cannot change too fast. The notes take <strong>$\eta=1/L$</strong>, which makes every step decrease the objective: $f(\mathbf{w}^{(t+1)})\le f(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})-\tfrac{\eta}{2}\|\nabla f(\mathbf{w}^{(t)})\|^2$.
30) <strong>A: plain gradient descent. B: stochastic gradient descent. C: gradient descent with momentum.</strong><ol><li><strong>A</strong> zig-zags cleanly back and forth across the steep direction and crawls along the shallow one &mdash; the signature of exact gradient steps on an ill-conditioned problem. The path is smooth and deterministic.</li><li><strong>B</strong> is jagged everywhere and never settles: each step uses one example (or a mini-batch), so the gradient is noisy and the iterates end up rattling around in a <em>noise ball</em> near the optimum rather than converging to it.</li><li><strong>C</strong> builds up velocity, which cancels the side-to-side oscillation and carries it quickly along the valley &mdash; so quickly that it <strong>overshoots</strong> the minimum and swings back before settling.</li></ol>
40) <strong>Gradient descent: $O(1/T)$. &nbsp;Nesterov’s accelerated gradient: $O(1/T^2)$.</strong>$$\text{GD},\ \eta=\tfrac1L:\qquad f(\mathbf{w}^{(T)})-f(\mathbf{w}^\star)=O\!\left(\frac{LR^2}{T}\right)$$$$\text{Nesterov}:\qquad f(\mathbf{w}^{(T)})-f(\mathbf{w}^\star)=O\!\left(\frac{LR^2}{T^2}\right)$$Both cost one gradient per step &mdash; momentum buys the extra factor of $T$ for free.
50) <ol><li><strong>GD:</strong> smooth convex objective, moderate $n$ and $d$, full-batch gradients affordable; you want a reliable monotone decrease.</li><li><strong>Momentum:</strong> ill-conditioned problems &mdash; a long narrow valley where plain GD zig-zags; the velocity cancels the oscillation and speeds up along the valley.</li><li><strong>SGD:</strong> $n$ very large or streaming data, where even one full gradient is too costly; the gradient noise can also help escape saddle points.</li><li><strong>Adagrad:</strong> sparse, high-dimensional features (text) where different coordinates need very different step sizes.</li></ol>
